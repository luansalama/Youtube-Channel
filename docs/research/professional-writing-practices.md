# Professional research synthesis: how strong stories are built

## 1. The central finding

There is **no academically established universal formula** for a great story. Three acts, five acts, the Hero’s Journey, Save the Cat, the Story Circle, eight sequences, and similar systems are **craft frameworks**, not natural laws.

Across classical poetics, modern narratology, cognitive research, professional screenwriting education, and platform analytics, the strongest convergence is this:

> A compelling story presents a meaningful change through a coherent chain of causes and consequences, driven by characters pursuing goals under pressure, while carefully controlling what the audience knows, expects, fears, and hopes for.

Aristotle’s foundational requirement was not a fixed number of beats, but a “whole” action whose beginning, middle, and end belong together rather than being an arbitrary collection of events. Modern research strengthens the causal part of this argument: events connected to the central causal chain are judged more important, remembered better, and selected more often in summaries. ([Internet Archive][1])

Professional institutions teach character, theme, conflict, world-building, visual storytelling, structure, treatments, rewriting, and feedback as an interconnected system. Sundance explicitly teaches both three-act structure **and when to depart from it**, which is the correct professional stance: structure should serve the material, not force every story into the same mould. ([collab.sundance.org][2])

# 2. The three layers writers must distinguish

A major source of weak planning is treating “story,” “plot,” and “script” as interchangeable.

## Story

The underlying events and changes in the fictional world:

* What happened?
* To whom?
* In what chronological order?
* What changed between the initial and final state?

Example:

> A peaceful village discovers an underground city. Its ruler offers protection, secretly intending to absorb the village into his empire.

## Plot

The selected and causally organised version of those events:

* Which events matter?
* What causes what?
* Where do reversals occur?
* What decisions make later events unavoidable?
* What information is withheld or revealed?

Plot is not merely “things that happen.” It is **the architecture connecting events**.

## Narrative discourse

How the audience experiences the plot:

* Where does the video begin?
* Whose perspective controls the information?
* Is an event shown directly, remembered, reported, or hidden?
* How much screen time does each event receive?
* What is revealed before or after its chronological position?

Narratology distinguishes the underlying events from their presentation, particularly through order, duration, frequency, viewpoint, and the act of narration. This distinction is essential for mysteries, flashbacks, unreliable narrators, parallel characters, cold opens, and reveal-based plots. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][3])

A harness should therefore store these separately:

```text
chronological_story
causal_plot
audience_presentation
```

# 3. The fundamental building blocks

## 3.1 The audience promise

Before the plot, establish the experience being promised.

Examples:

* “A builder discovers that his city is slowly being replaced.”
* “Five players must survive a kingdom in which death transfers memories.”
* “A villager realises that the world resets every seven days.”
* “A detective enters a server where every suspect has the same skin.”

The promise contains:

1. **Novel situation**
2. **Human or emotional problem**
3. **Source of dramatic pressure**
4. **Expected kind of pleasure**

That final element might be mystery, dread, wonder, comedy, tragedy, survival, strategic problem-solving, or emotional catharsis.

For YouTube, the title, thumbnail, opening, and story must make the **same promise**. YouTube’s official guidance states that the opening should immediately reassure viewers that the video will deliver the value or experience suggested by its packaging. ([Google Help][4])

### Diagnostic

> What specific experience would a viewer feel disappointed not to receive?

If that cannot be answered precisely, the concept is underdeveloped.

---

## 3.2 Premise

A premise is the dramatic configuration from which many events can grow.

A practical formulation:

> When **disruption** affects **protagonist**, they must pursue **difficult goal**, despite **central opposition**, or face **meaningful consequence**.

Example:

> When a perfect duplicate begins replacing members of his village, an isolated cartographer must identify the original residents before he becomes unable to prove that he is one of them.

A premise is stronger when it naturally generates:

* difficult decisions;
* escalating consequences;
* visual situations;
* conflict between characters;
* changes in relationships;
* multiple possible strategies;
* an irreversible climax.

A concept that only produces one interesting scene is not yet a durable premise.

---

## 3.3 Theme

Theme should not initially be written as a sermon.

Weak:

> “Friendship is important.”

Stronger thematic question:

> When does loyalty become complicity?

Or as competing values:

```text
loyalty versus truth
security versus freedom
memory versus identity
justice versus mercy
community versus individuality
control versus uncertainty
```

Theme becomes dramatic when different characters embody legitimate but incompatible responses.

For example:

* The protagonist believes safety requires control.
* The ally believes safety without freedom is imprisonment.
* The antagonist believes individual freedom inevitably destroys civilisation.
* The climax forces the protagonist to choose between these values.

Pixar’s educational material and Sundance’s professional curriculum both treat theme as connected to character needs, intention, emotional development, and structural decisions rather than as an isolated message. ([Khan Academy][5])

### Best practice

Write theme in three forms:

```text
thematic subject: identity
thematic question: are we defined by memory or choice?
provisional argument: identity is preserved through chosen commitments,
not merely through remembered history
```

The argument may change during drafting. The question should guide development without predetermining every answer.

## 3.4 Character

A useful character model needs more than biography.

For every principal character, define:

* **Want:** the conscious outcome they pursue.
* **Need:** the internal change that may allow them to succeed or live differently.
* **Fear:** the consequence they are structured to avoid.
* **Belief:** their interpretation of how the world works.
* **Misbelief or limitation:** where that interpretation becomes incomplete.
* **Strategy:** how they normally solve problems.
* **Contradiction:** the tension that prevents them from becoming one-dimensional.
* **Boundary:** what they currently refuse to do.
* **Pressure point:** what could force them across that boundary.
* **Relationship function:** what they reveal, challenge, threaten, or awaken in another character.
* **Agency:** decisions through which they alter the story.

Professional screenwriting instruction consistently centres protagonist wants and needs, obstacles, supporting-character functions, character-created conflict, intention, tactics, behaviour, and subtext. ([collab.sundance.org][2])

### Agency is more important than likeability

The protagonist does not need to be morally good or conventionally likeable. They need to be **legible and consequential**.

The audience should usually understand:

* what they currently want;
* why it matters to them;
* what they decide;
* what their decisions cause;
* what they may lose;
* what unresolved contradiction they carry.

Identification with characters is multidimensional rather than a simple like/dislike response. Audience engagement can arise through perspective, goals, emotions, moral evaluation, curiosity, similarity, or fascination. ([PMC][6])

---

## 3.5 Opposition and conflict

Conflict is not synonymous with combat.

Conflict exists when two conditions cannot both be satisfied.

Possible sources:

* characters want incompatible outcomes;
* characters want the same thing for different reasons;
* the world imposes limited resources;
* a character’s external goal conflicts with their moral boundary;
* the protagonist’s short-term strategy undermines their long-term need;
* truthful information threatens a valuable relationship;
* accomplishing the mission requires becoming more like the antagonist;
* the physical environment resists progress.

A useful opposition model:

```text
protagonist goal
opposing goal
why neither side can simply withdraw
advantage held by opposition
vulnerability held by opposition
cost of direct confrontation
cost of avoiding confrontation
```

The strongest antagonist is usually not an obstacle placed randomly in front of the protagonist. The antagonist is an **active causal force** with a strategy.

---

## 3.6 Stakes

Stakes answer:

> Why does the outcome matter?

Use multiple layers:

* **Immediate:** What can be lost in the current scene?
* **Practical:** What mission, home, resource, status, or relationship is threatened?
* **Personal:** Why does this affect this specific protagonist?
* **Relational:** Who will be betrayed, abandoned, exposed, or transformed?
* **Moral:** What principle must be compromised?
* **Existential:** What does failure say about who the protagonist is?
* **Systemic:** What larger group or world will be affected?

Increasing the number of deaths or explosions does not automatically increase dramatic power. Stakes become effective when they are specific, credible, understood, and connected to the character.

---

## 3.7 Causality

This is the most defensible structural principle in the research.

A strong story is not:

```text
A happens.
Then B happens.
Then C happens.
```

It is closer to:

```text
A causes B.
B forces a decision.
The decision produces C.
C prevents the previous strategy from working.
The character adapts, causing D.
```

Research on narrative comprehension found that events positioned within the main causal chain, and events with more causal connections, were more likely to be remembered, summarised, and judged important. ([ScienceDirect][7])

### The counterfactual test

For every major beat, ask:

> Had the previous event not occurred, would this event still have happened in substantially the same way?

If yes, the connection may be weak.

### The deletion test

> If this beat were removed, which later beats would become impossible or unintelligible?

If the answer is “none,” the beat may be decorative, repetitive, or misplaced.

Not every atmospheric moment must drive the mechanics of the plot, but it should earn its place through:

* character;
* mood;
* thematic development;
* world information;
* anticipation;
* contrast;
* setup;
* payoff.

---

## 3.8 Change

Narrative requires a meaningful transition between states.

Track change across several dimensions:

```text
external situation
knowledge
power
resources
relationships
moral position
identity
emotional condition
audience understanding
```

A scene can be quiet while still producing substantial change. Conversely, a large action scene may be dramatically static if it changes nothing except the number of enemies remaining.

---

## 3.9 Information design

The writer manages at least four audience states:

* **Knowledge:** what the audience currently understands.
* **Questions:** what remains unresolved.
* **Expectations:** what the audience predicts.
* **Emotional orientation:** what they want or fear will happen.

The principal effects include:

### Suspense

The audience anticipates an uncertain consequential outcome.

### Curiosity

The audience knows an important cause or explanation is missing.

### Surprise

An outcome violates an established expectation.

### Dramatic irony

The audience knows something a character does not.

### Mystery

The plot invites reconstruction of concealed events, motives, identities, or rules.

Suspense is associated with prediction, social cognition, emotional concern, and uncertainty management rather than merely withholding all information. ([PMC][8])

### Fair surprise

A strong twist should usually satisfy two tests:

1. It was difficult to predict.
2. After revelation, previous details acquire clearer meaning.

A twist that depends on information the story deliberately made impossible to infer may surprise the audience without satisfying them.

---

## 3.10 Payoff

Every major setup creates an implicit obligation.

Common setup types:

* object;
* rule;
* ability;
* weakness;
* warning;
* promise;
* lie;
* relationship tension;
* unusual visual detail;
* question;
* prophecy;
* moral boundary.

For each setup, record:

```text
setup
audience interpretation
planned payoff
payoff type
latest acceptable payoff point
status
```

Payoff types include:

* confirmation;
* reversal;
* escalation;
* reinterpretation;
* tragic fulfilment;
* comic return;
* character choice;
* visual transformation.

# 4. A flexible overall plot architecture

The following architecture is more useful than rigid percentages or mandatory page numbers.

## Movement 1: Promise and instability

Establish:

* the distinctive world or situation;
* the relevant protagonist;
* the emotional and genre contract;
* an unresolved instability.

For YouTube, do not confuse orientation with a long introduction. The opening should validate the title and thumbnail quickly. Official YouTube analytics identifies the first 30 seconds as a distinct retention period and recommends moving compelling material earlier when later sections outperform the opening. ([Google Help][9])

## Movement 2: Disruption

Something changes that cannot be ignored.

A disruption is effective when it:

* threatens a current value;
* creates a new desire or responsibility;
* reveals that the current strategy is inadequate;
* generates a concrete dramatic question.

## Movement 3: Commitment

The protagonist makes, accepts, or is trapped by a decision that creates the main line of action.

Before commitment, retreat may be easy.

After commitment:

* the cost of retreat rises;
* the opposition responds;
* the protagonist becomes causally responsible.

## Movement 4: Progressive complications

The protagonist pursues a strategy.

Each complication should do at least one of the following:

* increase the cost;
* reduce available options;
* reveal a deeper problem;
* produce unintended consequences;
* empower the opposition;
* divide allies;
* invalidate an assumption;
* force a more compromising tactic.

Escalation is not “more enemies every scene.” It is **less freedom under greater pressure**.

## Movement 5: Reversal or recontextualisation

Near the structural centre, something should substantially alter the audience’s or protagonist’s model of the story.

Possibilities:

* apparent victory exposes the real threat;
* the enemy is reidentified;
* the protagonist obtains the goal but discovers its cost;
* an ally’s motive is revealed;
* the rules of the world are reinterpreted;
* the protagonist becomes responsible for the disaster.

Aristotle identified reversal and recognition as especially powerful elements of complex plots. ([The Poetry Foundation][10])

A midpoint is useful when the material supports one. It is not mandatory merely because a formula requires it.

## Movement 6: Convergence and crisis

Subplots, relationships, world rules, and prior decisions converge.

The crisis is not simply the worst physical danger. It is the point at which the protagonist faces the story’s hardest meaningful choice.

A proper crisis usually forces a choice between values:

* save the city or expose its founding lie;
* retain power or preserve a friendship;
* defeat the antagonist using the antagonist’s methods or accept uncertainty;
* recover the past or protect the person created by forgetting it.

## Movement 7: Climax

The climax should resolve the central dramatic question through consequential action or choice.

The protagonist may:

* apply a changed understanding;
* repeat an old mistake;
* make a sacrifice;
* reject a false solution;
* integrate apparently incompatible values;
* become what they feared;
* recognise the truth too late.

The climax should depend on earlier causes. It should not be solved by a new ability, rule, ally, or object introduced at the last moment unless that arbitrariness is deliberately the thematic point.

## Movement 8: Resolution

Show the new equilibrium.

Resolve:

* the practical outcome;
* the central relationship;
* the protagonist’s changed state;
* the thematic consequence;
* the world’s reaction;
* the emotional aftertaste.

For serial storytelling, conclude the episode’s principal question before opening the next one. A cliffhanger should create anticipation, not replace resolution.

# 5. Scene construction

Every scene should have a reason to exist.

## The scene card

```text
scene_id:
location:
time:
point_of_view:
scene_purpose:

entry_state:
objective:
reason_objective_matters:
obstacle:
opposing_agent:
tactics:
information_revealed:
information_withheld:
emotional_progression:
visual_event:
turn:
outcome:
exit_state:
new_question:
setup_or_payoff:
production_requirements:
```

## The scene engine

A robust dramatic scene normally contains:

1. **Objective** — What does someone try to achieve now?
2. **Obstacle** — What prevents immediate success?
3. **Tactics** — How do they adapt?
4. **Turn** — What changes the direction or meaning?
5. **Outcome** — What is gained, lost, or made worse?
6. **New pressure** — Why must the story continue?

Sundance’s scene curriculum similarly focuses on objectives, obstacles, given circumstances, intention, tactics, beat shifts, dialogue, exposition, subtext, and transitions. ([collab.sundance.org][11])

## The scene-change rule

At the end, at least one important state should differ from the beginning:

* a decision has been made;
* information has changed hands;
* trust has risen or fallen;
* a resource has been acquired or lost;
* a threat has become immediate;
* a strategy has failed;
* a moral boundary has shifted;
* the audience has reinterpreted something.

## Enter late, leave with pressure

Usually begin near the point where meaningful friction starts.

Usually end after the decisive change, before the characters merely repeat or explain what the audience has understood.

This is an editing principle rather than a universal law. Deliberate slowness, ritual, atmosphere, or aftermath can be valuable when they perform a clear emotional or thematic function.

# 6. Dialogue and subtext

Dialogue should not be treated as the spoken version of an outline.

For every exchange, separate:

```text
literal words
speaker objective
speaker fear
tactic
concealed information
relationship status
power shift
subtext
```

Characters rarely need to state everything they know, feel, or want.

Dialogue becomes dramatic when:

* speakers pursue incompatible objectives;
* words perform actions such as persuading, threatening, testing, hiding, delaying, seducing, or provoking;
* the literal topic differs from the emotional issue;
* status shifts during the exchange;
* silence or avoidance becomes meaningful;
* speech reveals character through choice of tactic.

### Exposition

“Never use exposition” is bad advice.

The actual standard is:

* Is the information necessary now?
* Does the audience already understand it?
* Would the speaker naturally say it?
* Can it be delivered through conflict, discovery, behaviour, environment, or consequence?
* Does receiving the information change what someone does?

Exposition becomes weak when characters tell each other facts they both already know solely for the audience’s benefit.

# 7. The professional outlining stack

Not every project requires every document, but the harness should support the complete stack.

## Stage 1: Story brief

One page containing:

```text
working_title
format_and_target_length
genre
audience
core_promise
premise
thematic_question
emotional_destination
distinctive_world_mechanic
protagonist
central_opposition
climactic_choice
ending_state
```

## Stage 2: Logline

One or two sentences covering:

```text
protagonist
disruption
goal
opposition
stakes
distinctive hook
```

Do not overload it with every subplot or piece of lore.

## Stage 3: Character and relationship matrix

Store not only individual profiles but relational dynamics:

```text
character_a
character_b
public_relationship
private_tension
shared_history
what_a_wants_from_b
what_b_wants_from_a
power_balance
secret
predicted_change
```

## Stage 4: World bible

For Minecraft productions:

```text
geography
factions
political_order
economy_and_resources
technology_or_magic
gameplay_rules
death_and_respawn_rules
mob_behaviour
travel_constraints
communication_rules
history
myths
public_beliefs
hidden_truths
visual_language
canon_status
```

Every exceptional rule should record:

* who knows it;
* how it can be demonstrated;
* what it permits;
* what it prevents;
* what happens when it is broken.

## Stage 5: Ending design

Define early:

* final choice;
* climax mechanism;
* emotional result;
* thematic consequence;
* final relationship state;
* final image;
* unresolved serial thread.

This need not imprison the draft. It supplies a destination against which the causal chain can be tested.

## Stage 6: Causal spine

Create approximately the minimum number of events necessary to move from initial state to ending.

Each node records:

```text
event
cause
character_decision
consequence
new_information
state_change
later_events_dependent_on_it
```

Do this before adding decorative sequences.

## Stage 7: Sequence outline

A sequence is a cluster of scenes organised around a temporary objective.

```text
sequence_goal
strategy
primary_location
complications
reversal
outcome
story_state_afterward
```

## Stage 8: Beat sheet

The beat sheet contains concise dramatic changes, not complete prose.

Each beat should state:

```text
who acts
what they attempt
what interferes
what changes
why the next beat follows
```

## Stage 9: Scene list or scene cards

This is where pacing, location, visual feasibility, character presence, and production cost become visible.

## Stage 10: Treatment

A treatment converts the skeletal plot into a readable emotional narrative. Sundance explicitly distinguishes the plot-oriented outline from an emotion-driven treatment that incorporates motivation and feeling. ([collab.sundance.org][2])

## Stage 11: Screenplay

The screenplay should contain what can be:

* seen;
* heard;
* performed;
* edited;
* staged;
* animated.

Internal information must become behaviour, voice-over, visual design, dialogue, or deliberate ambiguity.

## Stage 12: Table read, storyboard, or animatic

This tests:

* clarity;
* rhythm;
* repetitive dialogue;
* confusing geography;
* emotional transitions;
* visual monotony;
* production difficulty;
* excessive exposition.

Pixar’s professional educational material explicitly places pitching, feedback, structure, character, visual language, and film grammar within the storytelling process. ([Khan Academy][12])

# 8. Revision should be divided into passes

Trying to repair plot, dialogue, continuity, grammar, pacing, and production feasibility simultaneously creates cognitive overload.

Professional writing research characterises writing as a demanding combination of planning, generating, and reviewing. Writing is recursive rather than a simple linear journey from plan to draft to edit. Research-based writing instruction also supports explicit planning, revision, clear goals, feedback, model analysis, and repeated practice. ([Cambridge University Press & Assessment][13])

Use separate passes.

## Pass 1: Promise

* Does the story deliver the concept?
* Does the opening represent the real video?
* Does the climax fulfil the central promise?

## Pass 2: Causality

* Does every major development arise from previous action?
* Are coincidences starting the story or repeatedly rescuing it?
* Which scenes have no downstream consequences?

## Pass 3: Character agency

* Who causes the major turns?
* Does the protagonist make consequential decisions?
* Is the antagonist active?
* Are allies independent people or delivery mechanisms?

## Pass 4: Escalation

* Are options narrowing?
* Are costs increasing?
* Do complications alter strategy?
* Is the same obstacle being repeated?

## Pass 5: Theme and character change

* Which decisions dramatise the thematic conflict?
* Does the climax force a value choice?
* Is the character’s change earned through pressure?

## Pass 6: Information

* What does the audience know at every point?
* Are mysteries intelligible?
* Are reveals prepared?
* Is confusion deliberate or accidental?
* Are explanations delivered too early?

## Pass 7: Scene necessity

For each scene:

* What is its function?
* What changes?
* Can it be removed?
* Can it be combined with another?
* Is it beginning too early or ending too late?

## Pass 8: Dialogue and subtext

* Can information become action?
* Are voices distinguishable?
* Is every character saying exactly what they feel?
* Does dialogue shift power or strategy?

## Pass 9: Visual storytelling

* Could the story still be understood with dialogue reduced?
* Are important rules demonstrated?
* Are locations visually distinct?
* Is the geography readable?
* Does the imagery evolve with the story?

## Pass 10: Production feasibility

* assets;
* rigs;
* skins;
* environments;
* simulations;
* lighting;
* crowd work;
* voice actors;
* render cost;
* editing complexity;
* music and sound requirements.

A beautiful scene that cannot be produced consistently should be redesigned before final scripting.

## Pass 11: Continuity and canon

Check:

* timelines;
* inventories;
* injuries;
* locations;
* character knowledge;
* world rules;
* established abilities;
* relationship states;
* unresolved setups.

## Pass 12: Audience results

After publication, classify retention changes rather than reacting vaguely.

Official YouTube reporting distinguishes:

* flat sections;
* gradual declines;
* spikes;
* dips;
* intro performance;
* top moments.

A spike may indicate enthusiasm or confusion; a dip may reflect pacing, expectation mismatch, repetition, weak clarity, or an uninteresting section. The graph identifies where to investigate, not automatically why the behaviour occurred. ([Google Help][9])

# 9. Specific principles for scripted Minecraft stories

## Make game mechanics causal

Minecraft should not merely decorate a story that could occur unchanged anywhere else.

Useful mechanics include:

* blocks storing memories;
* respawning altering identity;
* chunks disappearing;
* inventory limitations forcing betrayal;
* redstone systems controlling political power;
* coordinates functioning as forbidden knowledge;
* mobs obeying a hidden social hierarchy;
* sleep advancing an enemy’s plan;
* hardcore death creating permanent succession;
* construction changing territory or relationships.

The mechanic should influence:

* goals;
* strategies;
* conflict;
* reversals;
* climax;
* visual design.

## Build geography into the plot

The audience should understand:

* where safety is;
* where danger is;
* what separates locations;
* how long movement takes;
* which routes are blocked;
* how the map changes.

Spatial clarity makes pursuits, escapes, wars, discoveries, and strategic decisions meaningful.

## Use building as character action

Construction can reveal:

* values;
* grief;
* control;
* obsession;
* status;
* cooperation;
* deception;
* cultural difference.

A character who builds walls and a character who builds bridges are making different dramatic statements even before dialogue.

## Separate scripted inevitability from visible agency

The writer knows the endpoint. The characters must appear to reach it through decisions.

Do not write:

> The character enters the cave because the next scene occurs there.

Write:

> Because the character distrusts the official map, they follow the abandoned rail line, which leads into the cave and exposes the hidden settlement.

## Create an episodic engine

A sustainable series needs a source of repeatable story pressure.

Examples:

```text
Each episode reveals one consequence of a world that resets imperfectly.

Each mission recovers a lost district while exposing how the kingdom erased it.

Every new construction solves a survival problem but strengthens the entity beneath the city.

The group investigates one player’s disappearance while gradually learning
that the server itself chooses who is remembered.
```

The episode engine should generate new stories without resetting all character development.

# 10. What should and should not become hard rules

## Strong doctrine

These principles deserve automated checks:

* causal connections between major events;
* clear character goals;
* consequential decisions;
* opposition and constraints;
* meaningful state changes;
* setup/payoff tracking;
* consistency of world rules;
* controlled audience information;
* climax connected to prior choices;
* iterative revision;
* title-thumbnail-opening alignment;
* post-release analysis.

## Flexible professional conventions

These should produce warnings, not failures:

* three-act structure;
* midpoint;
* protagonist arc;
* scene objective;
* act climax;
* opening image and closing image;
* sequence length;
* number of subplots.

## Weak candidates for automation

Do not make these compulsory:

* exact page numbers;
* fixed percentages for every beat;
* exactly 15 beats;
* mandatory Hero’s Journey stages;
* a twist at a predetermined timestamp;
* a likeable protagonist;
* constant action;
* dialogue-free exposition;
* a mandatory happy ending;
* one prescribed emotional arc.

The reviewed institutional material teaches three-act structure alongside alternative structures and explicitly discusses when to break away from it. That is strong evidence against turning one template into a universal validator. ([collab.sundance.org][2])

# 12. The writing doctrine I would encode into the harness

The harness should not attempt to “generate a great story” in one prompt.

It should operate as a controlled sequence:

```text
research
→ concept generation
→ concept evaluation
→ premise
→ thematic model
→ character system
→ ending design
→ causal spine
→ sequence outline
→ beat sheet
→ scene cards
→ treatment
→ screenplay
→ table read / animatic
→ specialised revision passes
→ production validation
→ publication
→ evidence-based postmortem
```

Each stage should have:

* a defined input schema;
* a constrained output schema;
* acceptance criteria;
* adversarial review;
* human approval;
* version history;
* traceability to earlier decisions;
* explicit uncertainty;
* no silent canon changes.

The governing principle should be:

> AI may propose possibilities, but it must not silently decide canon, theme, character motivation, factual claims, or structural changes.

That preserves creative control and avoids dependence on generative randomness while still using AI for divergence, comparison, diagnostics, consistency checking, research, and revision.
