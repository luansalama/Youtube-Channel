# Direction — The Portal That Nobody Made

> This is the only document you need to read during Direction. The Studio Assistant maintains the supporting scorecard and research evidence internally.

## Development status

This is an **unlocked development direction**. It reflects the latest showrunner note but does not record a `direction_lock`. The time-travel model, ending principle and production boundaries are ready to evaluate; the exact Minecraft mechanism behind the destructive outcome still needs verification before Story is locked.

## Viewer promise

The viewer is promised a layered, grounded time-travel mystery told through a solitary Minecraft world. A lone player discovers a lit portal that nobody could have built, hesitates, and chooses to enter. That discovery is only the threshold: the main story follows his journey through displaced moments of his own world, where familiar places, unexplained damage and glimpses of another player gradually reveal that he is witnessing the consequences of his own actions from the wrong side of time.

The intended pleasure is not a last-minute twist delivered through exposition. It is the accumulating sensation that apparently separate mysteries are parts of one causal pattern. Attentive viewers should be able to suspect the truth before the protagonist confirms it, then reinterpret earlier images once they understand it.

The story would break its promise if the portal merely led to an ordinary Nether adventure, if time changed its rules to protect the twist, if an unseen cosmic force prevented choices, or if the ending depended on information that was withheld unfairly.

## Premise and hook

A lone player has established a modest survival homestead above a ravine. During an ordinary descent, he finds a complete, active portal in a section of cave he knows was empty. No other player has access to the world, and he did not build it.

He hesitates because the evidence contradicts what he knows, but curiosity and agency win: he enters during the opening movement. The story properly begins on the other side.

Instead of arriving in the expected Nether, he emerges into the same ravine in a different state. Blocks he remembers placing are absent; other damage has not happened yet; the route back to his home leads to a version of the landscape that cannot belong to the present. Successive crossings expose different moments rather than alternate worlds. Among them is a future in which his homestead has burned and his tamed wolf appears to be gone.

The initial question, **‘Who built the portal?’**, expands into two connected questions: **‘When am I?’** and **‘Can I prevent what I have already seen?’**

## Story engine and human problem

The protagonist is not simply carried through a puzzle. His decisions create its causal chain:

1. He enters the impossible portal despite his hesitation.
2. He investigates repeated locations instead of treating them as unrelated worlds.
3. He attempts to leave warnings and alter small events.
4. He discovers that those interventions produced details he already remembers.
5. He constructs the original portal so that his younger self will enter and gain the foreknowledge he believes may avert the future destruction.
6. When the destructive event arrives, he must choose between futilely protecting the homestead and using his limited time to rescue the wolf.

There is no conventional villain. The active opposition comes from the protagonist’s earlier and later selves acting with unequal information. His travelling self wants to break the observed chain; his older self needs the portal journey to occur because it created the only chance to act during the disaster. Their shared habits are an advantage when solving clues and a vulnerability when concealing identity. Avoiding the loop would cost the foreknowledge needed for the rescue; preserving it costs the home and the belief that the past can be rewritten.

The fixed timeline supplies constraint, not character motivation. Ordinary Minecraft causes, incomplete knowledge and the protagonist’s own choices must produce every apparent failure.

## Grounded time-travel model

The story uses a **single, self-consistent timeline** as a fictional world rule:

- There are no branches, resets or replacement timelines.
- The traveller experiences events in personal order while the world retains one chronological history.
- A past event that has already been observed cannot become a different event.
- The protagonist can still participate in the past and be one of the causes that made it happen.
- Each event happens once. Apparent repetition comes from seeing the same event at different points in the protagonist’s personal journey or from a different physical angle.
- No invisible force corrects paradoxes. A warning can fail because it is damaged, misunderstood or deliberately left incomplete; an intervention can become the remembered cause of the problem.
- Materials retain ordinary origins. The older protagonist mines or carries the obsidian and physically builds the portal. The loop does not conjure blocks or evidence from nowhere.
- The portal’s temporal behaviour is invented speculative canon, not a claim about vanilla Minecraft or real physics.

The protagonist initially interprets the experience as a recurring loop in which another attempt might change the result. The deeper revelation is that there have never been repeated versions. His journey has always been part of the one history he is trying to alter.

## Story, plot and audience presentation

**World chronology:** the homestead and relationship with the wolf exist first; the older, post-journey protagonist arrives before the discovery and constructs the portal; his younger self finds and enters it; the older self later confronts the already-observed destruction and saves the wolf while the homestead burns.

**Protagonist’s personal journey:** he finds the portal; visits later and earlier states of the world; witnesses fragments of his own past from outside; attempts to intervene; arrives before his original entry; builds the portal; watches his younger self enter; and finally reaches the destructive event with a different understanding of what can still be saved.

**Audience presentation:** the film follows personal rather than world chronology. Early evidence supports several readings. Spatial matches establish that this is one world before personal signatures establish whose actions are being observed. The older protagonist’s identity is confirmed through an action the audience has already seen the consequence of, not through an explanatory speech.

## Audience information design

| Layer | Audience question | Initial interpretation | Fair evidence | Pay-off |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Portal | Who built it? | Another player, an anomaly or an unknown survivor | Single-player isolation; familiar block placement; an asymmetrical cobblestone footing | The older protagonist physically built it before the opening |
| World state | Is this another world? | Future ruin, copy or simulation | The same unusual ravine profile, tree line and player-made details in different states | These are different moments of one world |
| Unseen figure | Who keeps interfering? | A hostile traveller or temporal echo | Matching movement habits, torch placement and equipment silhouette | Every sighting is the protagonist at another point in personal time |
| Repetition | Is he retrying the same events? | A reset loop with another chance | Damage and objects accumulate consistently; no event has two outcomes | Events occur once and are revisited from new perspectives |
| Outcome | Can the fire be prevented? | Foreknowledge should permit a better version | Each attempted safeguard becomes part of the remembered configuration | The homestead still burns, but the rescue was always part of the outcome |

Early clues should be legible without being uniquely identifying. Mid-story clues should make the self-loop a credible theory. Late evidence should confirm it by matching cause to an image already shown. The narrator must never lie; the narration stays close to what the protagonist understands at that point.

## Why Minecraft matters

1. **Single-player authorship:** A deliberately constructed portal in a private world creates an immediate contradiction. The player knows which permanent changes he made.
2. **Persistent blocks:** Missing blocks, repairs, torch placement, water paths and fire damage allow past actions to be read visually without dialogue.
3. **Repeatable geography:** The same ravine, cave mouth and homestead can be rebuilt in several temporal states. Viewers can recognise place before they understand time.
4. **Portal language:** Nether portals already imply crossing a boundary. Redirecting that expectation towards time creates surprise while remaining visually native to Minecraft.
5. **Survival causality:** Obsidian acquisition, route access, fire spread, water placement, animal movement and limited inventory can determine what the protagonist can attempt.
6. **Avatar identity:** Armour damage, tools, movement habits and a controlled silhouette can reveal that two apparent characters are the same person without requiring a second speaking role.

The exact cause of the homestead’s destruction must be a verified, reproducible Minecraft chain. It should interact with the protagonist’s precautions rather than occur as an arbitrary scripted explosion.

## Emotional and thematic direction

The intended movement is **impossibility → chosen curiosity → disorientation → pattern recognition → hope of intervention → self-recognition → tragic acceptance → purposeful action**.

The central thematic question is: **If an outcome cannot be rewritten, can a choice still matter?**

The answer should be dramatic rather than didactic. The protagonist cannot save the structure he set out to protect, but his foreknowledge lets him recognise the meaningful action still available. Saving the wolf does not create a new timeline; it reveals that what looked like total failure was always an incomplete reading of the future.

A second layer concerns authorship. The protagonist built the portal, but he copied the portal he remembered entering. The film may leave the viewer considering where the idea begins without treating that causal loop as a missing explanation.

## Narration and cast

- **Human voices:** one narrator, voiced by the creator.
- **Protagonist:** silent on screen; thoughts and interpretation are carried by narration.
- **Other human roles:** none required.
- **Optional borrowed voices:** at most one or two short lines, but they must be removable and must not carry essential clues, exposition or emotion.
- **Additional presence:** one tamed wolf provides a visual relationship and personal stake without another human performance.

The narrator may speak retrospectively, but the wording should preserve discovery. Phrases such as ‘I thought I was seeing the future’ are fair; phrases that knowingly conceal the narrator’s identity or final understanding are not.

## Ending direction

The mystery reveal and emotional resolution are connected but distinct.

The protagonist reaches the cave before his younger self’s discovery. He recognises the asymmetrical footing and other details from the opening because his own hurried construction is creating them. He completes and lights the portal, believing that his younger self must enter, see the future and carry the warning forwards. From concealment, he watches the opening hesitation again from the opposite side of the ravine. The younger self steps through. The ‘unknown builder’ is resolved without dialogue.

When the foretold destruction arrives, the protagonist’s preparations cannot produce a different history. The exact verified Minecraft chain still destroys the homestead. He finally stops spending the remaining moments on the building and rescues the wolf. After the fire, the landscape matches the ruin seen earlier; nothing has changed. What changes is the meaning of the image: the apparently empty future did not prove that every living thing was lost.

The final image is the protagonist leading the wolf away from the burned homestead at first light while the portal stands silent in the distance. The central mystery is answered, the fixed outcome is honoured, and the protagonist’s agency survives through the choice of what to save.

## Production scope

**Target duration:** approximately 20 minutes for development. It may expand only if clarity or earned emotion requires it; duration is not a structural quota.

**Scope:** medium, designed for a solo first production.

**Smallest version worth making:**

- One ravine-and-cave portal location in three readable states.
- One compact homestead and its burned state.
- One protagonist avatar, with younger and older appearances distinguished through equipment condition rather than a second performance.
- One tamed wolf.
- Narrator-only voice track.
- A limited number of temporal crossings, each changing the viewer’s understanding rather than merely repeating spectacle.

**Reusable elements:** portal set, ravine landmarks, homestead states, fire-damage palette, avatar continuity kit and temporal transition language.

**Primary risks:**

- Temporal geography may become confusing before it becomes intriguing.
- Showing two versions of one avatar may require controlled compositing, replay capture or carefully limited silhouettes.
- Resetting the same location across time states may create continuity errors.
- The destruction mechanism may fail to behave consistently in the chosen Minecraft version.
- The wolf can feel manipulative if the relationship is not established through ordinary shared actions before it becomes threatened.
- Twenty minutes may be too short if crossings are numerous; clarity takes priority over count.

**Scope controls:**

- Use no voiced supporting character.
- Use no parallel universe, time authority, lore council or explanatory scientist.
- Keep the mystery within one world, one relationship and one visible outcome.
- If a temporal visit does not add a clue, reverse an interpretation or force a decision, remove it.
- If the double-avatar capture is unreliable, restrict overlap to distant silhouettes and one controlled reveal shot.
- If an animatic cannot communicate the timeline with three world states, simplify the crossings before adding narration.

## Research decision

**Mode: light.** Two sources were consulted to distinguish changing a single past from participating in it and to understand self-consistency as a proposed constraint on closed-timelike-curve models. These sources support the internal logic used for the fiction; they do not establish that backwards time travel is physically achievable.

No films, episodes, novels or game narratives were used as plot references. This avoids importing another work’s reveal pattern, imagery or causal chain. Version-specific Minecraft research remains necessary once the destruction mechanism is selected.

## Direction decision record

**Proposed direction:** a grounded, self-consistent time-travel mystery in which the portal is the inciting gateway, the protagonist relives his own history from displaced perspectives, and his older self is revealed as the builder. The fixed destruction cannot be rewritten, but the protagonist’s final value choice remains consequential.

| Alternative | Status and rationale |
|---|---|
| Naturally generated portal with a mechanical explanation | Superseded. It resolves at the threshold and does not support the requested journey beyond the portal. |
| Branching timelines or repeated resets | Rejected for this direction. They weaken the fixed-outcome premise and make clue causality less precise. |
| Supernatural guardian enforcing history | Rejected. It adds exposition, another role and an arbitrary force where character action can carry causality. |
| Another player built the portal | Rejected. It breaks the isolation and removes the self-authorship layer. |
| Purely tragic ending in which nothing meaningful can be saved | Rejected. A fixed event should constrain agency, not make every choice irrelevant. |

**Approval status:** not locked. The homestead, fire and wolf are a concrete working stake proposed for evaluation and can still change before `direction_lock`.
