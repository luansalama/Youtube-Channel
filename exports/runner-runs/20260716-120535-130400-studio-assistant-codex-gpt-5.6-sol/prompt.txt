# Direction — The Portal That Nobody Made

> This is the only document you need to read during Direction. The Studio Assistant maintains the supporting scorecard and research evidence internally.

## Development status

This is an **unlocked development direction**. It reflects the latest showrunner note but does not record a `direction_lock`. The time-travel model, ending principle and production boundaries are ready to evaluate; the exact Minecraft mechanism behind the destructive outcome still needs verification before Story is locked.

## Viewer promise

The viewer is promised a layered, grounded time-travel mystery told through a solitary Minecraft world. A lone player discovers a lit portal that nobody could have built, hesitates, and chooses to enter. That discovery is only the threshold: the main story follows his journey through displaced moments of his own world, where familiar places, unexplained damage and glimpses of another player gradually reveal that he is witnessing the consequences of his own actions from the wrong side of time.

The intended pleasure is not a last-minute twist delivered through exposition. It is the accumulating sensation that apparently separate mysteries are parts of one causal pattern. Attentive viewers should be able to suspect the truth before the protagonist confirms it, then reinterpret earlier images once they understand it.

The story would break its promise if the portal merely led to an ordinary Nether adventure, if time changed its rules to protect the twist, if an unseen cosmic force prevented choices, or if the ending depended on information that was withheld unfairly.

## Premise and hook

A lone player has established a modest survival homestead above a ravine. During an ordinary descent, he finds a complete, active portal in a section of cave he knows was empty. No other player has access to the world, and he did not build it.

He hesitates because the evidence contradicts what he knows, but curiosity and agency win: he enters during the opening movement. The story properly begins on the other side.

Instead of arriving in the expected Nether, he emerges into the same ravine in a different state. Blocks he remembers placing are absent; other damage has not happened yet; the route back to his home leads to a version of the landscape that cannot belong to the present. Successive crossings expose different moments rather than alternate worlds. Among them is a future in which his homestead has burned and his tamed wolf appears to be gone.

The initial question, **‘Who built the portal?’**, expands into two connected questions: **‘When am I?’** and **‘Can I prevent what I have already seen?’**

## Story engine and human problem

The protagonist is not simply carried through a puzzle. His decisions create its causal chain:

1. He enters the impossible portal despite his hesitation.
2. He investigates repeated locations instead of treating them as unrelated worlds.
3. He attempts to leave warnings and alter small events.
4. He discovers that those interventions produced details he already remembers.
5. He constructs the original portal so that his younger self will enter and gain the foreknowledge he believes may avert the future destruction.
6. When the destructive event arrives, he must choose between futilely protecting the homestead and using his limited time to rescue the wolf.

There is no conventional villain. The active opposition comes from the protagonist’s earlier and later selves acting with unequal information. His travelling self wants to break the observed chain; his older self needs the portal journey to occur because it created the only chance to act during the disaster. Their shared habits are an advantage when solving clues and a vulnerability when concealing identity. Avoiding the loop would cost the foreknowledge needed for the rescue; preserving it costs the home and the belief that the past can be rewritten.

The fixed timeline supplies constraint, not character motivation. Ordinary Minecraft causes, incomplete knowledge and the protagonist’s own choices must produce every apparent failure.

## Grounded time-travel model

The story uses a **single, self-consistent timeline** as a fictional world rule:

- There are no branches, resets or replacement timelines.
- The traveller experiences events in personal order while the world retains one chronological history.
- A past event that has already been observed cannot become a different event.
- The protagonist can still participate in the past and be one of the causes that made it happen.
- Each event happens once. Apparent repetition comes from seeing the same event at different points in the protagonist’s personal journey or from a different physical angle.
- No invisible force corrects paradoxes. A warning can fail because it is damaged, misunderstood or deliberately left incomplete; an intervention can become the remembered cause of the problem.
- Materials retain ordinary origins. The older protagonist mines or carries the obsidian and physically builds the portal. The loop does not conjure blocks or evidence from nowhere.
- The portal’s temporal behaviour is invented speculative canon, not a claim about vanilla Minecraft or real physics.

The protagonist initially interprets the experience as a recurring loop in which another attempt might change the result. The deeper revelation is that there have never been repeated versions. His journey has always been part of the one history he is trying to alter.

## Story, plot and audience presentation

**World chronology:** the homestead and relationship with the wolf exist first; the older, post-journey protagonist arrives before the discovery and constructs the portal; his younger self finds and enters it; the older self later confronts the already-observed destruction and saves the wolf while the homestead burns.

**Protagonist’s personal journey:** he finds the portal; visits later and earlier states of the world; witnesses fragments of his own past from outside; attempts to intervene; arrives before his original entry; builds the portal; watches his younger self enter; and finally reaches the destructive event with a different understanding of what can still be saved.

**Audience presentation:** the film follows personal rather than world chronology. Early evidence supports several readings. Spatial matches establish that this is one world before personal signatures establish whose actions are being observed. The older protagonist’s identity is confirmed through an action the audience has already seen the consequence of, not through an explanatory speech.

## Audience information design

| Layer | Audience question | Initial interpretation | Fair evidence | Pay-off |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Portal | Who built it? | Another player, an anomaly or an unknown survivor | Single-player isolation; familiar block placement; an asymmetrical cobblestone footing | The older protagonist physically built it before the opening |
| World state | Is this another world? | Future ruin, copy or simulation | The same unusual ravine profile, tree line and player-made details in different states | These are different moments of one world |
| Unseen figure | Who keeps interfering? | A hostile traveller or temporal echo | Matching movement habits, torch placement and equipment silhouette | Every sighting is the protagonist at another point in personal time |
| Repetition | Is he retrying the same events? | A reset loop with another chance | Damage and objects accumulate consistently; no event has two outcomes | Events occur once and are revisited from new perspectives |
| Outcome | Can the fire be prevented? | Foreknowledge should permit a better version | Each attempted safeguard becomes part of the remembered configuration | The homestead still burns, but the rescue was always part of the outcome |

Early clues should be legible without being uniquely identifying. Mid-story clues should make the self-loop a credible theory. Late evidence should confirm it by matching cause to an image already shown. The narrator must never lie; the narration stays close to what the protagonist understands at that point.

## Why Minecraft matters

1. **Single-player authorship:** A deliberately constructed portal in a private world creates an immediate contradiction. The player knows which permanent changes he made.
2. **Persistent blocks:** Missing blocks, repairs, torch placement, water paths and fire damage allow past actions to be read visually without dialogue.
3. **Repeatable geography:** The same ravine, cave mouth and homestead can be rebuilt in several temporal states. Viewers can recognise place before they understand time.
4. **Portal language:** Nether portals already imply crossing a boundary. Redirecting that expectation towards time creates surprise while remaining visually native to Minecraft.
5. **Survival causality:** Obsidian acquisition, route access, fire spread, water placement, animal movement and limited inventory can determine what the protagonist can attempt.
6. **Avatar identity:** Armour damage, tools, movement habits and a controlled silhouette can reveal that two apparent characters are the same person without requiring a second speaking role.

The exact cause of the homestead’s destruction must be a verified, reproducible Minecraft chain. It should interact with the protagonist’s precautions rather than occur as an arbitrary scripted explosion.

## Emotional and thematic direction

The intended movement is **impossibility → chosen curiosity → disorientation → pattern recognition → hope of intervention → self-recognition → tragic acceptance → purposeful action**.

The central thematic question is: **If an outcome cannot be rewritten, can a choice still matter?**

The answer should be dramatic rather than didactic. The protagonist cannot save the structure he set out to protect, but his foreknowledge lets him recognise the meaningful action still available. Saving the wolf does not create a new timeline; it reveals that what looked like total failure was always an incomplete reading of the future.

A second layer concerns authorship. The protagonist built the portal, but he copied the portal he remembered entering. The film may leave the viewer considering where the idea begins without treating that causal loop as a missing explanation.

## Narration and cast

- **Human voices:** one narrator, voiced by the creator.
- **Protagonist:** silent on screen; thoughts and interpretation are carried by narration.
- **Other human roles:** none required.
- **Optional borrowed voices:** at most one or two short lines, but they must be removable and must not carry essential clues, exposition or emotion.
- **Additional presence:** one tamed wolf provides a visual relationship and personal stake without another human performance.

The narrator may speak retrospectively, but the wording should preserve discovery. Phrases such as ‘I thought I was seeing the future’ are fair; phrases that knowingly conceal the narrator’s identity or final understanding are not.

## Ending direction

The mystery reveal and emotional resolution are connected but distinct.

The protagonist reaches the cave before his younger self’s discovery. He recognises the asymmetrical footing and other details from the opening because his own hurried construction is creating them. He completes and lights the portal, believing that his younger self must enter, see the future and carry the warning forwards. From concealment, he watches the opening hesitation again from the opposite side of the ravine. The younger self steps through. The ‘unknown builder’ is resolved without dialogue.

When the foretold destruction arrives, the protagonist’s preparations cannot produce a different history. The exact verified Minecraft chain still destroys the homestead. He finally stops spending the remaining moments on the building and rescues the wolf. After the fire, the landscape matches the ruin seen earlier; nothing has changed. What changes is the meaning of the image: the apparently empty future did not prove that every living thing was lost.

The final image is the protagonist leading the wolf away from the burned homestead at first light while the portal stands silent in the distance. The central mystery is answered, the fixed outcome is honoured, and the protagonist’s agency survives through the choice of what to save.

## Production scope

**Target duration:** approximately 20 minutes for development. It may expand only if clarity or earned emotion requires it; duration is not a structural quota.

**Scope:** medium, designed for a solo first production.

**Smallest version worth making:**

- One ravine-and-cave portal location in three readable states.
- One compact homestead and its burned state.
- One protagonist avatar, with younger and older appearances distinguished through equipment condition rather than a second performance.
- One tamed wolf.
- Narrator-only voice track.
- A limited number of temporal crossings, each changing the viewer’s understanding rather than merely repeating spectacle.

**Reusable elements:** portal set, ravine landmarks, homestead states, fire-damage palette, avatar continuity kit and temporal transition language.

**Primary risks:**

- Temporal geography may become confusing before it becomes intriguing.
- Showing two versions of one avatar may require controlled compositing, replay capture or carefully limited silhouettes.
- Resetting the same location across time states may create continuity errors.
- The destruction mechanism may fail to behave consistently in the chosen Minecraft version.
- The wolf can feel manipulative if the relationship is not established through ordinary shared actions before it becomes threatened.
- Twenty minutes may be too short if crossings are numerous; clarity takes priority over count.

**Scope controls:**

- Use no voiced supporting character.
- Use no parallel universe, time authority, lore council or explanatory scientist.
- Keep the mystery within one world, one relationship and one visible outcome.
- If a temporal visit does not add a clue, reverse an interpretation or force a decision, remove it.
- If the double-avatar capture is unreliable, restrict overlap to distant silhouettes and one controlled reveal shot.
- If an animatic cannot communicate the timeline with three world states, simplify the crossings before adding narration.

## Research decision

**Mode: light.** Two sources were consulted to distinguish changing a single past from participating in it and to understand self-consistency as a proposed constraint on closed-timelike-curve models. These sources support the internal logic used for the fiction; they do not establish that backwards time travel is physically achievable.

No films, episodes, novels or game narratives were used as plot references. This avoids importing another work’s reveal pattern, imagery or causal chain. Version-specific Minecraft research remains necessary once the destruction mechanism is selected.

## Direction decision record

**Proposed direction:** a grounded, self-consistent time-travel mystery in which the portal is the inciting gateway, the protagonist relives his own history from displaced perspectives, and his older self is revealed as the builder. The fixed destruction cannot be rewritten, but the protagonist’s final value choice remains consequential.

| Alternative | Status and rationale |
|---|---|
| Naturally generated portal with a mechanical explanation | Superseded. It resolves at the threshold and does not support the requested journey beyond the portal. |
| Branching timelines or repeated resets | Rejected for this direction. They weaken the fixed-outcome premise and make clue causality less precise. |
| Supernatural guardian enforcing history | Rejected. It adds exposition, another role and an arbitrary force where character action can carry causality. |
| Another player built the portal | Rejected. It breaks the isolation and removes the self-authorship layer. |
| Purely tragic ending in which nothing meaningful can be saved | Rejected. A fixed event should constrain agency, not make every choice irrelevant. |

**Approval status:** not locked. The homestead, fire and wolf are a concrete working stake proposed for evaluation and can still change before `direction_lock`.

# Story plan — The Portal That Nobody Made

> This is an unlocked Story proposal. It develops the showrunner note without recording a creative lock, audience result or production validation.

## Development status

This revision replaces the rescue-choice version with a closed-loop tragedy. The protagonist never sees another version of himself. He first encounters the effects of his own future actions as environmental contradictions, enters the portal, builds a life with a tamed wolf in the same ravine at an earlier time, loses the wolf in a fire, and constructs the portal in a desperate attempt to go back again. A creeper kills him during an improvised lava ignition; the blast changes the lava-and-wood geometry, allowing ordinary fire spread to activate the frame. That is the portal his earlier personal self found.

The wolf’s working name is **Moss**. Moss has no pen or doghouse. The relationship is established through normal play: taming, travelling, gathering materials, feeding, building around an inquisitive companion, and using the sit command only when the protagonist enters the dangerous ravine mine.

The proposed world is Hardcore so the final death is terminal. The mode, exact Minecraft edition and mechanical chain remain unlocked until human approval and reproducibility testing.

## Audience promise

A lone player exploring an unvisited part of his private world finds a burned cabin and a lit portal that cannot belong there. The portal takes him not to the Nether but to the same untouched ravine several days earlier. As he survives, builds and bonds with a wolf, practical construction decisions begin producing the shapes he remembers from the ruin. The intended pleasure is recognising that every strange block, missing support and blast scar came from a normal action whose meaning was initially hidden.

The central questions are:

- Who built and lit the portal?
- Why does the new homestead become the ruin seen before it existed?
- Can foreknowledge prevent the fire and save Moss?

The story would break its promise if another player or distant silhouette supplied the mystery, if an invisible force corrected history, if Moss were kept in an invented enclosure, if the protagonist delayed rescue to save replaceable blocks, or if the portal lit merely because an explosion was treated as magical fire.

## Dramatic opening and relationship grounding

### Cold open

The film opens inside the later fire. The protagonist ignores a chest and burning wall, empties his water bucket towards the room where Moss is sitting, and breaks directly towards the wolf. Moss is visible through flame for a moment. The protagonist reaches forward; the image cuts before the outcome.

A restrained narration line may frame the promise without lying: **‘By the time I understood the portal, I was already late.’**

This fragment lasts roughly thirty-five seconds. It establishes immediate danger and makes clear that the protagonist is not choosing the house over the wolf.

### Return to the beginning

The film then moves to the protagonist’s earlier personal experience. During a solo expedition far from his established base, he reaches a ravine whose coordinates and map edge show that he has not visited it before. On the west rim stands the cobblestone remainder of a burned cabin. Its wooden upper structure is mostly absent. A stair descends towards a north-wall cave, where an active obsidian frame stands beside a fresh blast cavity, exposed lava and the remains of a plank ignition cradle.

There is no body, dropped collar, sign, book or glimpse of another avatar. The evidence is construction and damage. The protagonist inspects the blocks, confirms that the portal is real, and chooses to enter.

### Building the bond

The portal ejects him into the same cave several days earlier. The frame, cabin and player-placed stair do not exist. With night approaching and no return portal, he makes a temporary shelter on the only level shelf above the ravine.

A skeleton encounter supplies bones through ordinary gameplay. He uses them to tame a nearby wolf and calls it Moss. The shelter then grows into a compact spruce-and-cobblestone cabin through a short series of lived scenes rather than a decorative montage alone:

- Moss follows while the protagonist gathers spruce and repeatedly occupies the next block position during construction.
- The protagonist feeds Moss after ordinary damage and waits for the tail to rise before continuing.
- They return together at dusk; the protagonist closes the door, places food in the furnace and sits Moss beside the bed only before descending towards lava.
- On returning from the mine, he releases Moss immediately, and the wolf follows him through the next round of chores.
- A repeated wide shot records the cabin, the ravine stair and the two of them crossing the west ledge together.

These scenes give the later sit command emotional and mechanical meaning. It is a familiar safety routine, not confinement.

## Story in world chronology

1. The protagonist’s pre-travel self is several days away on a solo expedition. His later travelling self is ejected into the untouched ravine through a temporary portal plane. The two versions remain geographically separate and never see one another.
2. The travelling protagonist camps on the west shelf, tames Moss with locally obtained bones and gradually builds the spruce-and-cobblestone cabin, stone chest recess and ravine stair later seen as ruins.
3. Terrain dictates several distinctive decisions: the foundation bends around a granite seam, the stair anchors to a narrow stone rib, and one stripped-spruce support passes behind a stone-clad section above the lava shelf.
4. The protagonist recognises those shapes from the ruin he saw before entering the portal. He decides to leave with Moss at dawn. Before leaving, he tries to neutralise the lava beneath the stair and collect enough obsidian for a controlled return frame.
5. Moss is seated beside the bed because the lava shelf is dangerous. The protagonist pours water over the visible lava and mines the cooled blocks. Removing the final accessible block opens a concealed lateral lava flow. Water cools the face he can see, but the side flow reaches the hidden spruce support behind the stone cladding.
6. Fire travels through that continuous support and into separated parts of the cabin. The protagonist immediately abandons the structure, routes his single water source towards Moss and breaks through the shortest wall. He reaches the sitting place one damage interval too late. Moss dies in the fire; no collar or invented keepsake drops.
7. Following the missing-block path back down reveals that the fire, the surviving stone shell and the opening ruin are the same event. Grief converts the portal from a mystery into a proposed second chance.
8. Using the obsidian he mined, the protagonist builds the exact economical frame in the north-wall cave. The water-cooled cobblestone foot, asymmetry and remaining plank position reproduce the details from the opening.
9. His flint and steel and spare iron were lost with the cabin. Rather than pause to mine and smelt, he attempts an ordinary emergency ignition: lava outside the frame, with spruce positioned so a fire block may form inside it. Water used during the rescue has washed a torch from a lateral branch, leaving an approach dark.
10. A creeper wanders from that branch while the protagonist waits for the ignition. At close range it detonates and kills the already damaged protagonist. The explosion does not directly create fire. It breaks the cobblestone lip retaining the placed lava and removes part of the cradle. Lava reaches the remaining spruce; a subsequent fire spread places flame inside the valid frame and activates it.
11. The protagonist’s drops fall into lava or fire. The portal remains active beside the blast cavity. Later in world chronology, his earlier personal self reaches the ravine, finds the burned cabin and enters that same portal, beginning the journey just described.

## Personal journey and audience presentation

The film uses a cold-open fragment, then follows the protagonist’s personal order:

1. **Fire without context:** a brief image of the attempted rescue ends before Moss’s fate is known.
2. **The ruin before the home:** the protagonist explores the burned cabin and reads its surviving construction without knowing it is his work.
3. **The impossible threshold:** the active portal, blast cavity, cobblestone foot, exposed lava and incomplete ignition cradle are inspected. He enters by choice.
4. **The ravine before construction:** the same geography is pristine and the portal frame is absent. Persistent natural landmarks establish an earlier state rather than another world.
5. **A life made in the gap:** survival play, taming and building establish Moss and the cabin as lived experience.
6. **Recognition through design:** the granite notch, foundation bend, chest recess and stair rib reproduce the remembered ruin. The protagonist accepts that he saw the future of this home.
7. **A practical prevention plan:** he chooses departure with Moss, stone-clads the stair and attempts to remove the lava hazard before dawn.
8. **Prevention becomes cause:** obsidian mining releases the concealed side flow to the support hidden behind the firebreak.
9. **The cold open completed:** he gives up the structure immediately and races for Moss, but the established sit routine and fire timing make him too late.
10. **Grief becomes a new objective:** he traces the block path, understands the ruin and decides to rebuild the portal for another attempt.
11. **The builder revealed:** every opening detail is created through visible mining and placement. No speech or second avatar is needed.
12. **The portal nobody made:** the creeper kills him; altered lava geometry lights the frame; the final match cut returns to his earlier hand entering it. A possible final line is: **‘Nobody had built it before I found it. I built it afterwards.’**

The narrator may speak retrospectively but cannot conceal knowledge he already has within a scene. The cold open withholds an outcome by cutting away; it does not state a false result.

## Plot and causality

The major actions form one traceable chain:

- Exclusive authorship and an unvisited map region make the constructed ruin and active portal intolerable contradictions, so the protagonist enters.
- Entry strands him in the same ravine before any construction, so a temporary survival camp becomes necessary.
- Surviving together creates the relationship with Moss and produces the cabin whose remains he remembers.
- Terrain and efficient Minecraft building choices reproduce distinctive shapes from the ruin, allowing recognition through environment rather than another avatar.
- Recognition causes an active prevention plan: leave with Moss, interrupt the wooden route and neutralise the lava first.
- Sitting Moss keeps the wolf away from the lava operation but removes autonomous escape when the hidden side flow reaches timber.
- Mining the cooled lava both supplies the future portal and exposes the fire source.
- Moss’s death causes the protagonist to abandon his departure plan and construct the remembered portal immediately.
- The improvised ignition, washed-away torch and dark branch create the conditions for the creeper encounter.
- The explosion kills the builder and changes the lava geometry; ordinary fire spread then activates the portal found at the beginning.

There is no moment in which the protagonist debates saving stored blocks instead of Moss. Once Moss is threatened, every action is directed towards the wolf. The tragedy comes from timing, prior choices and Minecraft mechanics, not an implausible value decision.

Removing the relationship scenes makes the death merely functional. Removing the remembered ruin removes the reason for prevention. Removing the hidden support makes the fire arbitrary. Removing the sit routine permits a convenient escape. Removing the grief-driven portal decision breaks the title reveal. Removing the creeper and lava geometry leaves the initial activation unexplained.

## Characters and relationship

### The protagonist

He begins as a capable solo player who trusts observation, efficient building and preparation. His first goal is to identify the impossible portal and return to his own time. After becoming stranded, he creates a home and a relationship rather than treating the past as a puzzle room. Once the ruin matches his work, he believes enough careful construction can turn foreknowledge into control.

His strength is practical attention. His limitation is that grief makes the next technically possible action feel like the only tolerable action. After Moss dies, he does not pause to restore lighting, heal fully or obtain a safer ignition tool; he copies the remembered frame because it appears to promise one more attempt.

His important choices are to enter, remain near the portal site, tame Moss, build around the terrain, leave with Moss, neutralise the lava, mine the final block, abandon the house for the rescue, reconstruct the frame and attempt immediate ignition.

### Moss

Moss is a tamed companion with Minecraft-scale behaviour, not human reasoning. The wolf follows, waits when instructed, accepts food and cannot understand a future fire. Its reliable response to the sit command makes ordinary ravine work safer and later makes the fire lethal.

Moss is never placed in a pen. Its usual sitting place is beside the protagonist’s bed inside their shared cabin. The relationship moves from tentative taming to an easy work rhythm, then ends through a safety habit that cannot adapt without the player reaching it.

### Opposition and pressure

There is no secret intruder. The active opposition combines the protagonist’s grief-driven future objective with ordinary survival hazards. Before the fire he wants to preserve Moss and leave safely. After the loss he wants to force one more temporal attempt quickly. The creeper has a simple Minecraft goal once it detects him: approach and detonate. Darkness, low health, lost equipment and the protagonist’s focus on the ignition give it the advantage; lighting, distance, a shield or delay would be vulnerabilities, but his chosen haste removes those protections.

The fixed timeline describes the resulting history; it does not compel decisions or erase alternatives.

## Ending and meaning

The informational and emotional climaxes are the same causal event. Watching the protagonist build the opening frame answers the title question. Watching the creeper kill him while his failed ignition succeeds explains why the frame was active and why no builder remained.

The ending is tragic because the protagonist does choose Moss over the structure and still arrives too late. His failure is not moral indifference; it is the accumulated result of a sensible safety routine, an incomplete firebreak, a hazardous resource operation and a grief-driven refusal to pause. The proposed thematic argument is that foreknowledge does not automatically become control, and that grief can turn a possible tool into an instruction one feels unable to refuse.

The final image is the lit portal alone beside the blast cavity. A match cut brings back the earlier protagonist’s hand entering it. The physical loop closes, while the first origin of the portal’s temporal routing remains intentionally open. That open question may add reflective residue, but it cannot conceal any cause required for the home, fire, death or activation.

## Minecraft rules and production expression

### Ordinary mechanics intended to carry plot

- A tamed wolf follows when standing and remains where commanded when sitting.
- Bones used for taming come from an on-screen skeleton encounter.
- Water cools exposed lava and can wash away a torch.
- Mining an apparently safe cooled block can expose another lava source behind or below it.
- Lava can ignite nearby flammable blocks when fire spread is enabled.
- Fire can travel through a continuous hidden timber element into the cabin.
- A valid portal can activate when ordinary fire appears inside its frame.
- A creeper explosion breaks blocks and changes fluid access; it is not itself treated as a source of fire.
- A close creeper detonation can be terminal in the proposed Hardcore state.

### Speculative canon

The frame routes one entry from the later ruined ravine to the earlier pristine ravine and ejects the traveller where no standing frame yet exists. This is invented story canon, not a vanilla Minecraft or physics claim. Materials are not bootstrapped: obsidian, cobblestone, spruce, water and bones all have ordinary origins. Only the remembered frame arrangement and temporal routing lack an external first design.

### Capture approach

Most of the film should be acted through real recorded gameplay: exploration, mining, taming, feeding, building, sitting and releasing the wolf, water placement, fire response, portal construction and mob pressure. No simultaneous double-avatar shot is required. The special work is limited to temporal transitions, matched world states, the final observer angle on the death and the match cut that closes the loop.

## Evidence and approval still required

Before Story lock, production testing must establish the Minecraft edition, version, difficulty, Hardcore presentation and exact coordinates for:

- concealed lava flow after obsidian removal;
- fire spread from the hidden support to the cabin;
- water behaviour and torch removal;
- the time available to reach a seated wolf;
- reproducible wolf damage and death without invented blocking;
- creeper approach, blast radius and terminal player damage;
- blast modification of the lava-retaining lip;
- lava-to-spruce ignition and fire-based portal activation;
- destruction or concealment of ordinary item drops;
- persistence of the active portal until the opening discovery.

If any candidate behaviour is unreliable, the geometry should change while preserving the causal functions. No audience test, animatic result, fire test, wolf-pathing test or capture validation is claimed here.

# Continuity bible — The Portal That Nobody Made

## Canon authority

- Canon owner: Luan
- Current canon version: Story proposal 0.2 — unlocked, 16 July 2026
- Locked upstream artefacts: None stated. The supplied Direction is explicitly unlocked.
- Rule for proposed canon changes: record the proposal, dependency impact and human decision before altering locked work.
- Status of this bible: proposed continuity for human review; it does not record a Story lock, audience result or production validation.
- Major proposed changes: tragic wolf death, terminal protagonist death, Hardcore mode, no visible second self, no wolf enclosure, and a new worldline in which the opening ruin predates the protagonist’s personal arrival.

## Timeline

### World chronology

| World time | Objective event |
|---|---|
| W0 — earlier ravine arrival | The travelling protagonist is ejected through a temporary temporal plane into the pristine north-wall cave. No portal frame or cabin exists. His pre-travel self is several days away on a distant solo expedition and never enters this geography during W0–W4. |
| W1 — homestead period | The travelling protagonist camps on the west shelf, obtains bones from a skeleton, tames Moss and builds the spruce-and-cobblestone cabin, protected chest recess and ravine stair. The normal routine is to let Moss follow during safe work, feed it after damage, sit it beside the bed before lava work and release it on return. |
| W2 — recognition and prevention | The protagonist matches his foundation, granite notch, chest recess and stair rib to the burned ruin he remembers. He packs to leave with Moss at dawn, stone-clads the visible timber route and begins cooling the lava shelf. One stripped-spruce support remains continuous behind the cladding. |
| W3 — fire and loss | Mining the final visible cooled block opens a lateral lava flow. It reaches the concealed support and fire travels into the cabin. Moss is still sitting beside the bed. The protagonist directs every rescue action towards the wolf but reaches the interaction after terminal fire damage. Moss dies and the cabin burns to its opening state. |
| W4 — portal construction and death | The protagonist traces the fire, uses the locally mined obsidian to build the exact remembered frame and prepares a lava-and-spruce ignition. Rescue water has removed a torch from a lateral branch. A creeper approaches and kills him. The blast breaks the lava-retaining lip; subsequent lava ignition and fire activate the portal. Ordinary drops are destroyed by lava or fire. |
| W5 — discovery | The pre-travel protagonist reaches the ravine later in world chronology. He finds the burned cabin, blast cavity and active portal, sees no other self, and enters. The portal sends him to W0, where he becomes the travelling protagonist described above. |

### Protagonist’s personal order

P1 W5 burned-ravine discovery → P2 W5 portal entry → P3 W0 pristine ejection → P4 W1 cabin and Moss relationship → P5 W2 recognition and prevention → P6 W3 fire and Moss’s death → P7 W4 portal construction, creeper death and activation.

The film presents a short fragment from P6 before P1, then follows P1–P7. The fragment withholds Moss’s fate through a cut but does not present a different event.

### Version overlap

During W0–W4, the protagonist’s pre-travel version exists elsewhere in the same world on the distant route that will eventually reach the ravine at W5. The versions do not share a scene, line of sight, sound cue, item exchange or direct intervention. No double-avatar image is required.

### Closed causal information

The protagonist remembers the frame and later copies it. Obsidian, cobblestone, spruce, water, bones and equipment have ordinary origins. The physical matter is not bootstrapped. The first external origin of the frame arrangement’s temporal routing remains intentionally open in this proposal. It cannot be expanded into a supernatural agent or paradox-correction force without human approval.

## Relationship and power balance

The relationship begins through ordinary taming rather than instant sentiment. Moss follows on timber and food runs, receives food after damage, interferes with building in recognisable wolf fashion and waits beside the bed only when the protagonist descends towards lava. The protagonist releases Moss as soon as he returns.

The protagonist controls sit, follow and route choice. This is responsible play under normal conditions and the source of vulnerability during the fire. Once Moss is threatened, that vulnerability controls the protagonist’s time and route: he ignores the chest, abandons the structure and moves water solely towards the wolf.

Moss must not be shown in a pen, doghouse, kennel, closed decorative enclosure or separate pet room. The sitting place belongs to the shared cabin interior.

## Locations, geography, travel and blocking

### Fixed layout

- The ravine has a west-rim shelf, a north-wall recessed cave, a narrow stone rib and a recognisable granite seam.
- The cabin occupies the west shelf because it is the only practical level footprint near the absent portal site.
- The lower cobblestone foundation bends around the granite seam, producing an L-shaped surviving shell.
- A protected chest recess is cut into natural stone. It survives visually after the timber upper structure burns.
- The protagonist’s bed and Moss’s normal sitting position are in the shared main room, one short wall above the stair approach.
- A player-built stair descends from the cabin towards the north-wall portal cave.
- A lava shelf lies below the stair. The visible section can be cooled, while a concealed lateral source remains behind the final accessible block.
- One stripped-spruce support runs continuously behind a later stone-clad interruption and connects the lower route to cabin timber.
- A lateral cave branch approaches the portal site from beyond the protagonist’s immediate working area. Rescue water removes its torch before the final construction.
