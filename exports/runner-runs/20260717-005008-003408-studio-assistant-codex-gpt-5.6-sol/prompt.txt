# Direction — The Portal That Nobody Made

> This is the only document you need to read during Direction. The Studio Assistant maintains the supporting scorecard and research evidence internally.

## Development status

This is an **unlocked development direction**. It reflects the latest showrunner note but does not record a `direction_lock`. The time-travel model, ending principle and production boundaries are ready to evaluate; the exact Minecraft mechanism behind the destructive outcome still needs verification before Story is locked.

## Viewer promise

The viewer is promised a layered, grounded time-travel mystery told through a solitary Minecraft world. A lone player discovers a lit portal that nobody could have built, hesitates, and chooses to enter. That discovery is only the threshold: the main story follows his journey through displaced moments of his own world, where familiar places, unexplained damage and glimpses of another player gradually reveal that he is witnessing the consequences of his own actions from the wrong side of time.

The intended pleasure is not a last-minute twist delivered through exposition. It is the accumulating sensation that apparently separate mysteries are parts of one causal pattern. Attentive viewers should be able to suspect the truth before the protagonist confirms it, then reinterpret earlier images once they understand it.

The story would break its promise if the portal merely led to an ordinary Nether adventure, if time changed its rules to protect the twist, if an unseen cosmic force prevented choices, or if the ending depended on information that was withheld unfairly.

## Premise and hook

A lone player has established a modest survival homestead above a ravine. During an ordinary descent, he finds a complete, active portal in a section of cave he knows was empty. No other player has access to the world, and he did not build it.

He hesitates because the evidence contradicts what he knows, but curiosity and agency win: he enters during the opening movement. The story properly begins on the other side.

Instead of arriving in the expected Nether, he emerges into the same ravine in a different state. Blocks he remembers placing are absent; other damage has not happened yet; the route back to his home leads to a version of the landscape that cannot belong to the present. Successive crossings expose different moments rather than alternate worlds. Among them is a future in which his homestead has burned and his tamed wolf appears to be gone.

The initial question, **‘Who built the portal?’**, expands into two connected questions: **‘When am I?’** and **‘Can I prevent what I have already seen?’**

## Story engine and human problem

The protagonist is not simply carried through a puzzle. His decisions create its causal chain:

1. He enters the impossible portal despite his hesitation.
2. He investigates repeated locations instead of treating them as unrelated worlds.
3. He attempts to leave warnings and alter small events.
4. He discovers that those interventions produced details he already remembers.
5. He constructs the original portal so that his younger self will enter and gain the foreknowledge he believes may avert the future destruction.
6. When the destructive event arrives, he must choose between futilely protecting the homestead and using his limited time to rescue the wolf.

There is no conventional villain. The active opposition comes from the protagonist’s earlier and later selves acting with unequal information. His travelling self wants to break the observed chain; his older self needs the portal journey to occur because it created the only chance to act during the disaster. Their shared habits are an advantage when solving clues and a vulnerability when concealing identity. Avoiding the loop would cost the foreknowledge needed for the rescue; preserving it costs the home and the belief that the past can be rewritten.

The fixed timeline supplies constraint, not character motivation. Ordinary Minecraft causes, incomplete knowledge and the protagonist’s own choices must produce every apparent failure.

## Grounded time-travel model

The story uses a **single, self-consistent timeline** as a fictional world rule:

- There are no branches, resets or replacement timelines.
- The traveller experiences events in personal order while the world retains one chronological history.
- A past event that has already been observed cannot become a different event.
- The protagonist can still participate in the past and be one of the causes that made it happen.
- Each event happens once. Apparent repetition comes from seeing the same event at different points in the protagonist’s personal journey or from a different physical angle.
- No invisible force corrects paradoxes. A warning can fail because it is damaged, misunderstood or deliberately left incomplete; an intervention can become the remembered cause of the problem.
- Materials retain ordinary origins. The older protagonist mines or carries the obsidian and physically builds the portal. The loop does not conjure blocks or evidence from nowhere.
- The portal’s temporal behaviour is invented speculative canon, not a claim about vanilla Minecraft or real physics.

The protagonist initially interprets the experience as a recurring loop in which another attempt might change the result. The deeper revelation is that there have never been repeated versions. His journey has always been part of the one history he is trying to alter.

## Story, plot and audience presentation

**World chronology:** the homestead and relationship with the wolf exist first; the older, post-journey protagonist arrives before the discovery and constructs the portal; his younger self finds and enters it; the older self later confronts the already-observed destruction and saves the wolf while the homestead burns.

**Protagonist’s personal journey:** he finds the portal; visits later and earlier states of the world; witnesses fragments of his own past from outside; attempts to intervene; arrives before his original entry; builds the portal; watches his younger self enter; and finally reaches the destructive event with a different understanding of what can still be saved.

**Audience presentation:** the film follows personal rather than world chronology. Early evidence supports several readings. Spatial matches establish that this is one world before personal signatures establish whose actions are being observed. The older protagonist’s identity is confirmed through an action the audience has already seen the consequence of, not through an explanatory speech.

## Audience information design

| Layer | Audience question | Initial interpretation | Fair evidence | Pay-off |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Portal | Who built it? | Another player, an anomaly or an unknown survivor | Single-player isolation; familiar block placement; an asymmetrical cobblestone footing | The older protagonist physically built it before the opening |
| World state | Is this another world? | Future ruin, copy or simulation | The same unusual ravine profile, tree line and player-made details in different states | These are different moments of one world |
| Unseen figure | Who keeps interfering? | A hostile traveller or temporal echo | Matching movement habits, torch placement and equipment silhouette | Every sighting is the protagonist at another point in personal time |
| Repetition | Is he retrying the same events? | A reset loop with another chance | Damage and objects accumulate consistently; no event has two outcomes | Events occur once and are revisited from new perspectives |
| Outcome | Can the fire be prevented? | Foreknowledge should permit a better version | Each attempted safeguard becomes part of the remembered configuration | The homestead still burns, but the rescue was always part of the outcome |

Early clues should be legible without being uniquely identifying. Mid-story clues should make the self-loop a credible theory. Late evidence should confirm it by matching cause to an image already shown. The narrator must never lie; the narration stays close to what the protagonist understands at that point.

## Why Minecraft matters

1. **Single-player authorship:** A deliberately constructed portal in a private world creates an immediate contradiction. The player knows which permanent changes he made.
2. **Persistent blocks:** Missing blocks, repairs, torch placement, water paths and fire damage allow past actions to be read visually without dialogue.
3. **Repeatable geography:** The same ravine, cave mouth and homestead can be rebuilt in several temporal states. Viewers can recognise place before they understand time.
4. **Portal language:** Nether portals already imply crossing a boundary. Redirecting that expectation towards time creates surprise while remaining visually native to Minecraft.
5. **Survival causality:** Obsidian acquisition, route access, fire spread, water placement, animal movement and limited inventory can determine what the protagonist can attempt.
6. **Avatar identity:** Armour damage, tools, movement habits and a controlled silhouette can reveal that two apparent characters are the same person without requiring a second speaking role.

The exact cause of the homestead’s destruction must be a verified, reproducible Minecraft chain. It should interact with the protagonist’s precautions rather than occur as an arbitrary scripted explosion.

## Emotional and thematic direction

The intended movement is **impossibility → chosen curiosity → disorientation → pattern recognition → hope of intervention → self-recognition → tragic acceptance → purposeful action**.

The central thematic question is: **If an outcome cannot be rewritten, can a choice still matter?**

The answer should be dramatic rather than didactic. The protagonist cannot save the structure he set out to protect, but his foreknowledge lets him recognise the meaningful action still available. Saving the wolf does not create a new timeline; it reveals that what looked like total failure was always an incomplete reading of the future.

A second layer concerns authorship. The protagonist built the portal, but he copied the portal he remembered entering. The film may leave the viewer considering where the idea begins without treating that causal loop as a missing explanation.

## Narration and cast

- **Human voices:** one narrator, voiced by the creator.
- **Protagonist:** silent on screen; thoughts and interpretation are carried by narration.
- **Other human roles:** none required.
- **Optional borrowed voices:** at most one or two short lines, but they must be removable and must not carry essential clues, exposition or emotion.
- **Additional presence:** one tamed wolf provides a visual relationship and personal stake without another human performance.

The narrator may speak retrospectively, but the wording should preserve discovery. Phrases such as ‘I thought I was seeing the future’ are fair; phrases that knowingly conceal the narrator’s identity or final understanding are not.

## Ending direction

The mystery reveal and emotional resolution are connected but distinct.

The protagonist reaches the cave before his younger self’s discovery. He recognises the asymmetrical footing and other details from the opening because his own hurried construction is creating them. He completes and lights the portal, believing that his younger self must enter, see the future and carry the warning forwards. From concealment, he watches the opening hesitation again from the opposite side of the ravine. The younger self steps through. The ‘unknown builder’ is resolved without dialogue.

When the foretold destruction arrives, the protagonist’s preparations cannot produce a different history. The exact verified Minecraft chain still destroys the homestead. He finally stops spending the remaining moments on the building and rescues the wolf. After the fire, the landscape matches the ruin seen earlier; nothing has changed. What changes is the meaning of the image: the apparently empty future did not prove that every living thing was lost.

The final image is the protagonist leading the wolf away from the burned homestead at first light while the portal stands silent in the distance. The central mystery is answered, the fixed outcome is honoured, and the protagonist’s agency survives through the choice of what to save.

## Production scope

**Target duration:** approximately 20 minutes for development. It may expand only if clarity or earned emotion requires it; duration is not a structural quota.

**Scope:** medium, designed for a solo first production.

**Smallest version worth making:**

- One ravine-and-cave portal location in three readable states.
- One compact homestead and its burned state.
- One protagonist avatar, with younger and older appearances distinguished through equipment condition rather than a second performance.
- One tamed wolf.
- Narrator-only voice track.
- A limited number of temporal crossings, each changing the viewer’s understanding rather than merely repeating spectacle.

**Reusable elements:** portal set, ravine landmarks, homestead states, fire-damage palette, avatar continuity kit and temporal transition language.

**Primary risks:**

- Temporal geography may become confusing before it becomes intriguing.
- Showing two versions of one avatar may require controlled compositing, replay capture or carefully limited silhouettes.
- Resetting the same location across time states may create continuity errors.
- The destruction mechanism may fail to behave consistently in the chosen Minecraft version.
- The wolf can feel manipulative if the relationship is not established through ordinary shared actions before it becomes threatened.
- Twenty minutes may be too short if crossings are numerous; clarity takes priority over count.

**Scope controls:**

- Use no voiced supporting character.
- Use no parallel universe, time authority, lore council or explanatory scientist.
- Keep the mystery within one world, one relationship and one visible outcome.
- If a temporal visit does not add a clue, reverse an interpretation or force a decision, remove it.
- If the double-avatar capture is unreliable, restrict overlap to distant silhouettes and one controlled reveal shot.
- If an animatic cannot communicate the timeline with three world states, simplify the crossings before adding narration.

## Research decision

**Mode: light.** Two sources were consulted to distinguish changing a single past from participating in it and to understand self-consistency as a proposed constraint on closed-timelike-curve models. These sources support the internal logic used for the fiction; they do not establish that backwards time travel is physically achievable.

No films, episodes, novels or game narratives were used as plot references. This avoids importing another work’s reveal pattern, imagery or causal chain. Version-specific Minecraft research remains necessary once the destruction mechanism is selected.

## Direction decision record

**Proposed direction:** a grounded, self-consistent time-travel mystery in which the portal is the inciting gateway, the protagonist relives his own history from displaced perspectives, and his older self is revealed as the builder. The fixed destruction cannot be rewritten, but the protagonist’s final value choice remains consequential.

| Alternative | Status and rationale |
|---|---|
| Naturally generated portal with a mechanical explanation | Superseded. It resolves at the threshold and does not support the requested journey beyond the portal. |
| Branching timelines or repeated resets | Rejected for this direction. They weaken the fixed-outcome premise and make clue causality less precise. |
| Supernatural guardian enforcing history | Rejected. It adds exposition, another role and an arbitrary force where character action can carry causality. |
| Another player built the portal | Rejected. It breaks the isolation and removes the self-authorship layer. |
| Purely tragic ending in which nothing meaningful can be saved | Rejected. A fixed event should constrain agency, not make every choice irrelevant. |

**Approval status:** not locked. The homestead, fire and wolf are a concrete working stake proposed for evaluation and can still change before `direction_lock`.

# Story plan — The Portal That Nobody Made

> This is an unlocked Story proposal. It records no creative lock, audience result or production validation.

## Development status

This revision adopts the new echo-reality journey and replaces the previous fixed-timeline tragedy within the unlocked development material.

The protagonist and Moss arrive together at a clearing above a ravine. Beneath it, they discover an active portal made from crying obsidian, although such a frame should not work like an ordinary Minecraft portal. The first crossing confuses the protagonist. After exploring the same ravine in a changed reality, he turns back expecting the portal to undo the trip. Instead, it advances him and Moss into another version of the same place. Further crossings reveal that the gate is a one-way chain rather than a reversible door.

A damaged version of the protagonist’s own survey journal, found in an abandoned echo, explains enough of the invented portal behaviour to support the climax. When the chain finally strands them in a habitable reality, they build a home beside the ravine. A wool rug placed too close to an open fireplace later catches fire while the protagonist is away collecting the last materials needed for a controlled return. He comes home too late and Moss is gone.

The protagonist uses the journal’s instructions to build a complete crying-obsidian frame in the ravine. He discharges a respawn anchor to wake it, knowing the activation is explosive but believing the frame will open before the blast reaches him. The explosion kills him. The portal lights a fraction too late.

The camera enters the active frame, rises through the corresponding underground reality and breaks through the surface into the exact scene presented after the cold open: the protagonist and Moss first reaching the clearing and deciding to stay. The portal he will soon discover already waits below them. The ending therefore closes the physical, emotional and presentational loop without claiming that the protagonist survived the blast.

The portal rules, fire geometry, target Minecraft edition and explosion staging remain proposed until human approval and reproducibility testing.

## Audience promise

A lone player and his wolf discover a portal nobody could have built in an untouched part of their private world. Crossing it does not take them to the Nether. It carries them through increasingly unsettling realities of the same ravine, and trying to go back only moves them farther from the world they knew.

The intended pleasure is cumulative recognition:

- the same geography acquiring incompatible histories;
- a return attempt becoming another departure;
- crude marks proving that the realities are distinct rather than changing around him;
- an older version of his own journal turning an abstract anomaly into a dangerous possibility;
- a comfortable household detail becoming the fire’s ordinary cause;
- the crying frame at the end becoming the impossible portal from the beginning;
- the final ascent revealing that the apparent beginning was always the portal’s destination.

The central questions are:

- Who made the crying-obsidian portal beneath the clearing?
- Why does going back through it lead somewhere new?
- Can the protagonist use what he learns to return to a reality where Moss is still with him?
- Does opening a route matter if he cannot survive long enough to cross it?

The story would break its promise if the realities were interchangeable spectacle, if a hidden wizard or visible second player supplied the explanation, if the rug fire lacked a legible material path, if Moss were forgotten while the house burned, or if the final explosion were presented as a verified vanilla method for activating crying obsidian.

## The selected knowledge source: The Weeping Atlas

The strongest working solution is not a generic ancient lore book. It is a weathered version of the protagonist’s own survey journal, found in the Archive Echo.

At the beginning, the protagonist carries a small field journal in which he records coordinates and draws a distinctive three-line symbol for ravines. In the Archive Echo, he finds an older journal with the same repaired corner, the same shorthand and the same hand. It contains observations apparently made by another version of him:

- several plans of the identical ravine laid over one another;
- a diagram showing that an unanchored crossing follows the next arrow, never the previous one;
- the phrase **ONE FRAME, MANY MOUTHS**;
- a full crying-obsidian frame connected to a respawn anchor;
- an arrow from that frame to **FIRST THRESHOLD**;
- a warning that the hand which discharges the anchor becomes the key;
- a blast-distance page whose final measurement has been torn away.

The book does not explain the universe or introduce a lore authority. Its diagrams record experiments, failed returns and practical observations. The protagonist verifies the one-way rule through his own crossings before trusting the final instruction.

The final surface reveal shows the younger protagonist carrying the clean, mostly blank version of the same journal. This pays off the book’s identity while leaving the exact history of the counterpart who completed it open. That unanswered authorship is reflective residue, not missing information required to understand the fire, the explosion or the loop.

### Reserve approaches if the Atlas proves too explicit

| Option | Dramatic advantage | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| An abandoned echo laboratory | Item frames, marked portals and redstone indicators could demonstrate the rule without prose | Requires a larger set and implies an unseen experimenter without the personal journal pay-off |
| A cartographic palimpsest | Layered maps reveal the same coordinates and crying particles trace a route to the first map | More mysterious and visually elegant, but the respawn-anchor danger may become too obscure |
| A ruined-portal field manual | A concise written book gives the rule directly | Cheapest to stage, but least personal and most dependent on exposition |

The Weeping Atlas is the working choice because it joins information, character identity, visual evidence and the final image without adding another speaking role.

## Proposed echo-portal canon

These are invented story rules, not claims about vanilla Minecraft or real physics.

1. Echoes are stable realities with the same underlying coordinates but divergent histories. They are not resets, dreams or temporary illusions.
2. A crying-obsidian frame binds matching coordinates across echoes. The nearest safe vertical exit may differ, but the ravine profile remains the primary orientation landmark.
3. An unanchored crossing moves forwards through a chain of echoes. Turning round and entering again does not reverse the previous crossing.
4. Blocks placed beside a portal belong only to that reality. A missing cobblestone mark therefore provides fair evidence that the traveller has not returned.
5. A projected mouth can appear in an echo where nobody locally placed the frame. Its material source is the constructed frame in the reality that created the connection.
6. A complete crying-obsidian frame can be keyed to the builder’s first threshold by discharging a charged respawn anchor within it.
7. The builder must trigger that discharge directly. The proposed Overworld discharge is explosive; the frame’s response to it is speculative canon.
8. Activation does not rewrite a reality. It opens a route to another existing echo at the moment bound to the first threshold.
9. The final R4 frame projects the receiving mouth found in R0. The R0 protagonist enters that receiving mouth without a key, so the one-way chain carries him onwards to R1 rather than back to R4.
10. The camera may cross the completed connection after the protagonist dies, but this is audience access rather than evidence that his consciousness survives.

The film should teach only the rules needed for the current decision. Geography, repeated portal composition, adjacent block marks and the Atlas diagrams carry most of the explanation.

## Dramatic opening

### Cold open

At night, the protagonist returns over the ridge carrying crying obsidian and glowstone. His house is already burning. He drops the materials, runs past recoverable storage and empties his water bucket towards the shared room. The wool rug beside the fireplace is aflame. Moss cannot be seen from the doorway.

The protagonist breaks through the shortest wall and reaches into the smoke. The scene cuts before the room is revealed.

A restrained narration line may establish the emotional promise:

**By the time I saw the smoke, Moss had been alone in it for too long.**

The fragment should last about thirty-five seconds. It withholds Moss’s fate but makes the protagonist’s priority unambiguous.

### Return to the beginning

Morning replaces the fire. The protagonist and Moss reach a green clearing above a recognisable ravine. They are already an established pair: Moss follows naturally, waits while the protagonist checks the map and sits at the overlook while he opens his clean survey journal.

The repeated narration line is simple:

**Moss sat down before I did. I took it as a vote. We could stay.**

He draws the ravine symbol, places a temporary marker and hears a low portal hum below. Their decision to investigate produces the first crossing. At this point, the audience reads the scene as an ordinary chronological beginning. The ending later reveals it as the receiving reality created by the final portal.

## Protagonist’s personal journey and audience presentation

1. **Fire without an answer:** The cold open shows the protagonist returning too late to an established home and racing towards an unseen Moss.
2. **A place worth keeping:** Earlier in his experience, he and Moss reach the clearing, mark it in the survey journal and decide to stay.
3. **The portal nobody made:** In the ravine they find a complete, active crying-obsidian frame in an unvisited private world. No path, mine, camp or dropped inventory identifies a builder.
4. **The first crossing:** They enter together and emerge beside the same granite seam in the Rain Echo. The weather, vegetation and water paths are incompatible with the clearing they left.
5. **The failed return:** Confused, the protagonist explores long enough to verify the geography, then re-enters the portal expecting to go home. It sends them to the Ash Echo instead.
6. **A method replaces panic:** He marks the frame’s left foot with cobblestone, records coordinates and crosses once more. The next mouth has no mark. Same place and different histories become the best explanation.
7. **The Weeping Atlas:** In the Archive Echo, an abandoned survey recess contains the older version of his journal. Its diagrams name the one-way behaviour and propose a dangerous method of reaching the first threshold.
8. **The last uncontrolled crossing:** Environmental pressure and a failing portal plane force one more entry. They emerge in a habitable echo. The local mouth closes and cannot be relit with ordinary fire. Without a complete frame, sufficient crying obsidian or a charged anchor, they cannot leave.
9. **A life in one reality:** They remain near the ravine because it is the only known seam between echoes. A shelter becomes a house. The protagonist builds a stone fireplace with an open flame and places a wool rug beside it for Moss. Their routines turn enforced settlement into home.
10. **Preparing a route without abandoning the present:** Over time, the protagonist collects crying obsidian and studies the Atlas. He delays the dangerous activation until he has a full frame, glowstone and a plausible blast shelter.

## Plot and causality

The major chain is:

- The clearing offers shelter and the portal hum threatens the protagonist’s confidence that the region is untouched, so he investigates.
- The complete crying frame presents the only direct explanation for its own impossibility, so he enters with Moss rather than leaving the wolf behind.
- The first changed reality confuses him, but repeated geography makes the portal the most credible route home.
- Re-entering advances instead of returning, so he begins marking coordinates and adjacent blocks.
- Missing marks and incompatible world histories establish a chain of echoes, so the Atlas can clarify observed evidence rather than invent the premise late.
- The Atlas and failing mouth force a final uncontrolled crossing, which strands them in R4.
- Staying near the only known seam leads to the house, hearth and wool rug.
- The protagonist’s gradual portal research requires crying obsidian and glowstone, causing the supply journey during which the continuously burning hearth reaches the rug.
- Sitting Moss indoors is an established safety choice for dangerous travel, but it prevents following or teleporting while the protagonist is away.
- The fire and Moss’s disappearance turn a cautious future project into an immediate attempt to reach the first threshold.
- The Atlas’s direct-hand rule requires the protagonist to remain close to the respawn anchor discharge.
- The explosion kills him, but its discharge activates the crying frame.
- The portal’s destination reveals the opening reality and creates the mouth his earlier self will enter.

Removing the failed return removes the central portal rule. Removing the marks makes the reality conclusion arbitrary. Removing the Atlas removes the protagonist’s basis for building and activating the final frame. Removing the domestic Moss scenes makes the rug and loss merely functional. Removing the wool adjacency makes the fire arbitrary. Removing the material-gathering objective removes the reason he is away. Removing the anchor blast breaks the protagonist’s death and the portal’s activation. Removing the repeated surface shot breaks the final loop.

## Characters and relationship

### The protagonist

The protagonist is observant, practical and accustomed to treating Minecraft systems as legible. He begins with healthy curiosity: when the portal contradicts his map, he investigates. His first mistake is understandable rather than foolish. He assumes that a portal can be traversed in reverse because ordinary doors and familiar Nether travel support that expectation.

His strength is evidence gathering. After the failed return, he marks blocks, records coordinates and compares terrain. His limitation emerges gradually: once knowledge offers even a dangerous possibility of reversal, he finds it harder to accept the reality in front of him.

His important decisions are to stay at the clearing, enter with Moss, attempt the return, continue marking echoes, trust the Atlas only after verifying part of it, settle near the dead mouth, build a home, pursue the materials slowly, leave Moss somewhere normally safe, search after the fire, build the crying frame and directly discharge the anchor.

His final choice is not between Moss and the house. The house is already lost. He chooses a lethal chance of reaching a reality with Moss over surviving in a reality where Moss may be gone.

### Moss

Moss is already the protagonist’s tamed companion when the story begins. The relationship is expressed through Minecraft-scale behaviour rather than human reasoning:

- Moss follows while the protagonist surveys the clearing;
- waits while he bridges or enters hazardous areas;
- interrupts construction by occupying useful blocks;
- receives food after ordinary damage;
- sleeps or sits on the wool rug beside the hearth;
- remains home only during the dangerous final supply run.

Moss is not kept in a pen, kennel or separate pet room. The rug is part of the shared living space. Its placement begins as an affectionate domestic choice and becomes the fire’s vulnerable material.

The film confirms that Moss is absent after the fire but does not show a body, invented collar drop or on-screen death message. The protagonist treats that absence as loss. The final R0 image shows an earlier Moss, not proof that the R4 Moss escaped.

### Opposition and pressure

There is no conventional villain. Active pressure comes from incompatible objectives within the protagonist:

- his practical self wants evidence, preparation and a survivable activation;
- his grief-driven self wants the first available route to a reality where Moss is alive.

The echo system has a one-way advantage. The Atlas is incomplete. The fire has destroyed time, shelter and emotional patience. Direct activation keeps the protagonist inside the blast problem. He could delay, rebuild, seek better protection or live without certainty, but every delay feels like choosing the Moss-less reality.

The costs remain concrete. Careful preparation costs emotional acceptance of the present. Immediate activation costs his life.

## Ending and meaning

The informational climax is the activation of the full crying frame. The protagonist has physically built the portal, supplied the crying obsidian, charged the anchor and triggered the event that projects its earlier mouth. Nobody built the portal in R0, but the protagonist built the connected frame in R4 after he had already found it in his own personal order.

The emotional climax is his decision to wake it before he has a verified way to survive. He is not careless about Moss or distracted by possessions. He has searched, found the rug empty and concluded that the only reality in which Moss is certainly alive lies behind the first threshold. The tragedy is that the method works a fraction too late for its builder.

After the blast, the camera remains. Purple light gathers between the crying blocks. It passes through the frame, travels vertically through dark stone and soil, and emerges into morning.

The exact Return to the beginning composition appears. Moss sits at the overlook. The earlier protagonist opens the blank journal and draws the ravine symbol. The earlier narration repeats:

**Moss sat down before I did. I took it as a vote. We could stay.**

A faint portal hum rises from below. The audience knows they will investigate, enter, attempt to return, cross the echoes, build the house and eventually create the connection beneath them.

The ending argues that reaching another reality is not the same as recovering what was lost. The protagonist succeeds in opening the route but cannot turn that technical success into a second life. Home remains meaningful because he and Moss chose and made it together, even though that choice also begins the loop.

Moss’s exact fate in R4 remains intentionally unseen in this proposal. If Story lock requires explicit death rather than absence, that must be a human decision and the fire blocking should be revised without inventing a non-Minecraft corpse or keepsake.

# Production plan — The Portal That Nobody Made

> This is the practical plan for building, recording, and handing material to the edit. Internal shot, asset, location, capture, and risk registers are maintained for you.

## Portal implementation — research candidate

The portal effect is technically plausible with existing mods, but the combined stack is not yet production-validated. Do not treat this section as a mod lock until the proof of concept passes and its exact versions are recorded.

### Candidate baseline

- Minecraft Java Edition 1.20.1
- Fabric loader profile dedicated to this production
- Immersive Portals 5.2.0 for Minecraft 1.20.1
- Multiworld 1.12 for Fabric
- Dimensional Weather 1.1-1.20.1
- Compatible Fabric API and iCommon API builds pinned after the clean-profile launch test

No mod jar is stored in the project. Download from the official pages recorded in `.studio/internal/research/source-ledger.csv`, then record the filenames, versions and file hashes in the asset register and build log.

### Echo-world architecture

Create R0, R1, R2, R3 and R4 as separate saved overworld-like worlds. Use:

- the same seed;
- the same Minecraft version;
- the same generator;
- the same generation-affecting mods and data packs;
- the same target coordinates for the hero ravine and portal mouth.

Different seeds are not the default because the script requires the same ravine profile, lightning-shaped granite seam, cave silhouette and surface composition. The story requires different histories, not unrelated geography.

For exact continuity, generate and verify one clean master location before art direction. Back it up, then duplicate the world or required region files for each echo. Apply vegetation, water, ash, archive structures, homestead construction and destruction only after the copies exist.

### Portal topology

Use an Immersive Portals portal entity inside each crying-obsidian frame. Point it to the matching coordinates in the next echo world. The intended route is:

```text
R0 → R1 → R2 → R3 → R4
```

Keep each normal crossing one-way unless a shot explicitly requires a return pair. Do not depend on a naturally generated Nether-style portal link. The crying-obsidian frame, particles, sound, keyed behaviour, anchor activation and creeper-triggered ignition remain scripted production effects rather than documented vanilla or mod mechanics.

### Weather design

Use dimension-specific weather so R0 can be dry while R1 is raining. The proof of concept must confirm that the following are visible and stable through the opening:

- rain particles;
- destination sky and light level;
- waterfall and wet-side geometry;
- destination ambience or a controllable post-production substitute;
- correct dry-side weather around the camera.

### Proof-of-concept gate: R0 to R1

Run this before building the complete echo chain:

1. Create a clean, isolated Minecraft 1.20.1 Fabric profile.
2. Install only the candidate stack and its required libraries.
3. Record every jar filename and version.
4. Create two overworld-like worlds from the same seed.
5. Verify the hero coordinates in both worlds.
6. Set R0 dry and R1 raining.
7. Build one temporary portal and assign R1's matching coordinates as its destination.
8. Record a static view, lateral parallax move and continuous player crossing.
9. Test a tamed wolf crossing in both follow and sit/release states.
10. Save, exit, reload and repeat the view and crossing.
11. Add the intended camera, replay, shader and capture tools one at a time.
12. Record results, logs, screenshots and capture filenames in the build log.

### Acceptance criteria

The portal implementation may be locked only when:

- the destination renders clearly through the frame;
- perspective and parallax remain convincing;
- the crossing has no loading screen or visible teleport cut;
- separate dry and rainy weather states coexist;
- the wolf crosses without ownership or AI corruption;
- the portal remains intentionally one-way;
- save/reload preserves worlds and portal destinations;
- the selected capture stack does not crash or corrupt the world;
- performance is sufficient for the intended recording workflow;
- a backup can restore the last known-good state.

### Fallback ladder

Use the least invasive fallback that preserves the scene:

1. Disable shaders or incompatible visual mods during portal plates.
2. Capture clean dry-side and wet-side plates separately and composite the portal interior in post.
3. Use duplicated matched sets rather than live independently generated chunks.
4. Transfer Moss in a separate controlled plate and hide the join with blocking or sound.
5. Replace the live one-way chain with manually configured portals for each shot.
6. Research a custom compatibility or portal-controller mod only after the recorded proof of concept identifies a specific unsolved failure.
